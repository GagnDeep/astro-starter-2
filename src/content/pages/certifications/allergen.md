  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is food allergen awareness training?"
    text_content: |
      Food allergen awareness training is a specialized educational program that teaches food service staff how to identify the major food allergens, communicate risks to guests, and prevent dangerous cross-contact during preparation and service. Unlike general food safety, allergen training focuses entirely on managing the nine major allergens identified by the FDA, including peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, and sesame.

      In many states, allergen awareness certification is now legally mandated for all managers and highly recommended for frontline staff. The training emphasizes that while pathogens can be killed by cooking, allergen proteins cannot.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do caterers manage cross-contact at events?"
    text_content: |
      Caterers manage cross-contact at events by using dedicated prep stations, color-coded cutting boards, and entirely separate serving utensils for allergen-free meals, ensuring that a trace amount of an allergen never touches a safe dish. In a chaotic off-site environment, this requires strict protocols, such as plating allergen-free meals first and sealing them before the rest of the food is handled.

      Cross-contact often happens silently on buffets. Guests use the same tongs for the pasta salad and the gluten-free dish. Caterers must train staff to monitor buffets aggressively or opt for plated service for guests with severe allergies.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What are the Big Nine food allergens?"
    text_content: |
      The Big Nine food allergens, which account for over ninety percent of all severe allergic reactions in the United States, are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame. Sesame was added to this list by the FASTER Act, requiring mandatory labeling and strict cross-contact prevention protocols.

      Caterers must aggressively track these ingredients down to the sub-recipe level. A minor dash of Worcestershire sauce (contains fish) or a hidden splash of soy sauce (contains wheat and soy) can trigger a life-threatening anaphylactic reaction.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How should caterers label allergens on buffets?"
    text_content: |
      Caterers should label allergens on buffets by placing clear, legible tent cards in front of every single dish, explicitly listing any of the major allergens contained within, rather than just marking a dish as "safe." Transparency allows guests to make informed decisions and reduces the risk of accidental exposure during rapid service.

      Do not rely on servers to memorize every ingredient in every dish for a massive buffet. Visual labeling is the last line of defense against an allergic reaction in a chaotic catering environment.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is the liability for serving an allergen to a guest?"
    text_content: |
      The liability for serving an allergen to a guest who explicitly disclosed their allergy is catastrophic, exposing the caterer to massive personal injury lawsuits, criminal negligence charges, and immediate revocation of their health permit. Standard general liability insurance policies may not cover gross negligence if you blatantly ignore documented safety protocols.

      Document every allergen request in writing on the Banquet Event Order (BEO) and ensure it is signed off by the lead chef. Verbal communication on the event floor is insufficient and highly dangerous.
