    heading: "How should you structure wedding catering packages?"
    text_content: |
      You should structure wedding catering packages using a tiered good-better-best model, clearly defining what is included in the base per-person price and what constitutes an upgrade. A base package must cover the menu, standard service staff, and basic equipment, while upgrades include premium proteins, late-night stations, signature cocktails, and high-end tabletop rentals.

      Never present a single, massive lump sum. Clients need to understand the value of the components. Break the quote down into food, beverage, labor, rentals, and the administrative fee. This transparency prevents sticker shock and allows clients to adjust their headcount or selections to meet their budget rather than demanding a flat discount.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you calculate labor costs for a wedding?"
    text_content: |
      You calculate labor costs for a wedding by establishing exact staffing ratios based on the service style, mapping the entire timeline from load-in to strike, and charging an hourly rate that covers payroll taxes and insurance. For a plated dinner, budget one server for every ten to twelve guests; for a buffet, one server for every twenty to twenty-five guests.

      Do not bury labor costs in the food price. Quote labor as a separate line item. If a wedding runs two hours over schedule, you need a mechanism to bill the client for that extended labor. A separate labor line item makes this contractual obligation clear.

    heading: "Should you charge for wedding menu tastings?"
    text_content: |
      You should charge for wedding menu tastings to filter out unqualified leads and cover the substantial food and labor costs involved in producing sample dishes. The standard practice is to charge a flat fee for the tasting, which is then credited toward their final invoice if they sign the contract and pay the deposit.

      Tastings are expensive marketing events. Providing them for free to anyone who inquires guarantees you will waste weekend hours feeding people who have no intention of booking.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you handle dietary restrictions in wedding pricing?"
    text_content: |
      You handle dietary restrictions in wedding pricing by building a small buffer into the overall per-person base price to cover the specialized ingredients and separate prep required for common allergies or vegan requests. Never charge the couple a petty surcharge per vegan meal, as it creates friction; absorb it in the average.

      However, if a client requests an entirely separate, highly complex menu for a subset of guests (e.g., an entirely separate kosher kitchen prep), this requires a distinct, premium contract line item.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Should you offer discounts for children and vendors?"
    text_content: |
      You should offer discounted meals for children and event vendors, typically pricing them at fifty percent of the adult rate for children and providing a simple, hot boxed meal for vendors at a flat rate of twenty-five to thirty-five dollars. Vendors do not drink alcohol or eat appetizers, and children consume significantly smaller portions.

      Failing to offer these industry-standard accommodations can cost you the contract. It also builds goodwill with photographers and planners, who are critical referral sources for future high-end wedding business.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How should you price late-night wedding snacks?"
    text_content: |
      You should price late-night wedding snacks at a premium margin, as they require your staff to stay on-site an additional two hours and manage a secondary service period right when exhaustion peaks. Never throw in late-night snacks for free to close a deal; they are highly requested and carry immense perceived value.

      Charge per person based on a realistic consumption rate (typically fifty to seventy percent of the original guest count will partake) and ensure the labor extension is explicitly covered in the quote.
