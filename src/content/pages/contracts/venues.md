  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is a catering venue commission?"
    text_content: |
      A catering venue commission is a fee, usually ranging from ten to twenty percent of the food and beverage total, that a caterer pays to an exclusive or preferred venue in exchange for the right to operate on their property. Venues justify this fee by claiming they provide the lead, maintain the facility, and handle the primary marketing for the event space.

      While legal and standard in the industry, commissions represent a massive hit to a caterer's bottom line. If your net profit margin is twelve percent, absorbing a fifteen percent venue commission means you are paying to cater the event.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you pass venue fees to the client?"
    text_content: |
      You pass venue fees to the client by adding a specific line item on the invoice, clearly labeled as a "Venue Administrative Fee" or "Venue Commission Surcharge," ensuring the client absorbs the cost rather than letting it erode your margins. Transparency is critical; clients need to understand that this fee is mandated by their chosen venue, not an arbitrary caterer markup.

      Never hide the commission by quietly inflating your per-person food costs. If a client compares your proposal for this venue against a proposal for a non-commission venue, the inflated food costs will make you look uncompetitive and dishonest.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What are kitchen use fees and trash removal policies?"
    text_content: |
      Kitchen use fees are flat-rate charges venues impose on caterers for utilizing on-site prep areas, while trash removal policies dictate whether the caterer must physically haul garbage off the property at the end of the night. Both must be explicitly detailed in the venue agreement and passed along in the client's contract to avoid surprise operating expenses.

      Many historic venues prohibit caterers from using their dumpsters, meaning you must load soiled trash bags into your vans and pay for commercial disposal back at your commissary. This labor and disposal cost must be accounted for in your pricing.

      Review how this ties into your client contracts in our [Contracts Hub](/contracts/). To ensure your business model can sustain these fees, revisit [Average Catering Profit Margins](/pricing/margins/).
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do preferred vendor lists impact catering sales?"
    text_content: |
      Preferred vendor lists impact catering sales by providing a steady stream of highly qualified, high-converting leads, as clients who book the venue are often contractually obligated to choose from that list. Securing a spot on these lists drastically reduces your external marketing costs, though you trade that for the venue commission fee.

      Getting on a list requires building strong relationships with venue managers, demonstrating flawless execution, and never leaving the venue dirty. One damaged wall during load-in can get you permanently removed from a lucrative list.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Can you negotiate catering venue commissions?"
    text_content: |
      You can negotiate catering venue commissions if you bring significant volume to the venue or agree to provide in-kind services, such as catering the venue’s internal staff holiday party at cost in exchange for a lower percentage on client events. Negotiation is difficult for new caterers but possible once you establish a track record.

      Always ensure the commission is calculated on the food and beverage subtotal only, explicitly excluding labor, rentals, and sales tax. Paying a commission on your staff's wages is a fatal margin error.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is a catering buy-out fee at a venue?"
    text_content: |
      A catering buy-out fee is a substantial penalty charged to a client when they choose to hire an outside, non-preferred caterer rather than using the venue’s exclusive in-house team. This fee, often ranging from twenty to thirty dollars per guest, compensates the venue for the lost revenue they would have earned from their own food and beverage operation.

      Caterers must clearly communicate to prospective clients that if they are bringing you into a restrictive venue, the client is responsible for paying the buy-out fee directly to the property.
