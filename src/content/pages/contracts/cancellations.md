    heading: "What makes a catering deposit non-refundable?"
    text_content: |
      A catering deposit is legally non-refundable only if the contract explicitly states it is non-refundable, defines it as a "retainer" to secure the date, and clearly outlines that the fee covers the administrative cost of turning down other business. Courts often strike down vague "deposit" clauses if they appear to be punitive rather than a fair estimate of actual damages suffered by the caterer.

      To strengthen enforceability, avoid calling the initial payment a deposit. Label it a "Retainer and Liquidated Damages Fee." State plainly that by reserving this date, you are declining other potential revenue, and the fee compensates you for that lost opportunity.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you structure a sliding-scale cancellation policy?"
    text_content: |
      You structure a sliding-scale cancellation policy by tying the refund amount inversely to the proximity of the event date, ensuring that as your hard costs increase, the client's potential refund decreases. A standard scale might allow a fifty percent refund of paid amounts (excluding the retainer) if canceled ninety days out, but mandate full payment if canceled within fourteen days.

      This structure protects the caterer as they begin ordering specialty ingredients, scheduling staff, and committing rental equipment. Once perishable goods are purchased, the client must be on the hook for those costs regardless of the event's status.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is a force majeure clause in catering?"
    text_content: |
      A force majeure clause in catering is a contractual provision that relieves both parties from liability or obligation when an extraordinary event or circumstance beyond their control prevents one or both parties from fulfilling their obligations. Examples include hurricanes, pandemics, acts of terrorism, or government-mandated lockdowns that render the event legally or physically impossible.

      Crucially, a force majeure clause does not mean the client gets a full refund immediately. A well-drafted clause should state that the caterer will apply paid funds toward a rescheduled date, rather than returning cash that has already been spent on event prep.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you handle weather-related catering cancellations?"
    text_content: |
      You handle weather-related catering cancellations by outlining a specific inclement weather clause that requires the client to secure an indoor contingency plan or a rated tent, rather than allowing them to cancel and demand a refund. If the client fails to provide safe shelter for the food and staff, the caterer reserves the right to halt service without refund.

      Weather is an inherent risk of outdoor events. Your contract must clarify that rain does not constitute a force majeure event unless it reaches the level of a declared natural disaster.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Can a caterer cancel a contract legally?"
    text_content: |
      A caterer can legally cancel a contract if they include a "Right of Refusal" or "Caterer Cancellation" clause that permits termination if the client breaches payment schedules, exhibits abusive behavior towards staff, or demands illegal service practices (like serving minors). In these cases, the retainer is typically forfeited by the client.

      If the caterer must cancel due to their own unforeseen emergency (e.g., a total kitchen fire), the contract should dictate a full refund of all monies paid and, ideally, a commitment to help source an alternative vendor.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Are non-refundable catering deposits actually legal?"
    text_content: |
      Non-refundable catering deposits are legal in most jurisdictions provided they are carefully drafted as a "retainer" or "liquidated damages" rather than a penalty. Courts recognize that caterers turn away other lucrative business to hold a specific date. If the client cancels, the caterer suffers real financial harm that cannot easily be recouped.

      However, if you charge a $10,000 non-refundable deposit for an event that is canceled a year in advance, a judge might deem it excessive and unenforceable. The amount must reasonably reflect the actual loss.
