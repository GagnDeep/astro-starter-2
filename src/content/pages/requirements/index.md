    text_content: |
      Under the EU Battery Regulation, starting February 18, 2027, all LMT (Light Means of Transport) batteries, industrial batteries with a capacity above 2 kWh, and electric vehicle batteries must have an electronic record—a battery passport. This digital passport is not merely a label, but a comprehensive data repository that tracks the battery throughout its lifecycle.

      The requirements are sweeping and unprecedented in global manufacturing. They demand a profound shift in how supply chains operate, moving from opaque linear models to transparent, circular systems. This shift is driven by the urgent need to ensure the sustainability of batteries, given their critical role in the transition to renewable energy and electric mobility.

      ### What are the core obligations?

      The regulation imposes a wide array of obligations, but they broadly fall into a few key categories:

      1.  **Data Collection and Reporting:** Manufacturers must collect and report detailed information on the battery's composition, origin, performance, and carbon footprint. This data must be accessible via a QR code linked to the passport.
      2.  **Supply Chain Due Diligence:** Companies must implement rigorous due diligence policies to identify and mitigate social and environmental risks in their supply chains, particularly concerning raw materials like cobalt, lithium, and nickel.
      3.  **Recycled Content:** By specific dates (e.g., 2031 and 2036), new batteries must contain minimum percentages of recycled cobalt, lead, lithium, and nickel.
      4.  **Carbon Footprint:** Batteries must meet maximum life cycle carbon footprint thresholds. This requires detailed calculation and verification of emissions at every stage, from mining to manufacturing.

      The complexity of these requirements cannot be overstated. They require unprecedented coordination across complex, global supply chains. A manufacturer in Germany must now verifiable data from a mine in the Democratic Republic of Congo or a refinery in China.

      ### Who is responsible?

      The regulation places the primary responsibility on the "economic operator" placing the battery on the market or putting it into service for the first time within the EU. This typically means the manufacturer, the importer, or a distributor. If a company outside the EU wants to sell its batteries in the European market, it must ensure a battery passport is created and maintained.

      This concept of extended producer responsibility is central to the EU's approach. It ensures that the entities profiting from the sale of batteries are also responsible for their environmental impact throughout their lifecycle, including end-of-life management and recycling.

      ### The Importance of Verification

      Crucially, the data within the battery passport must be verified by an independent third party—a "notified body." Self-declarations are not sufficient. This verification process ensures the integrity and reliability of the data, preventing greenwashing and ensuring a level playing field.

      The notified body will audit the manufacturer's systems and data, ensuring they comply with the stringent requirements of the regulation. This adds a significant layer of complexity and cost to the compliance process, requiring companies to invest in robust data management and traceability systems.

      ### Carbon Footprint Calculation

      One of the most immediate and challenging requirements is the calculation of the battery's carbon footprint. This is not a simple estimate; it requires a detailed lifecycle assessment (LCA) based on specific rules and methodologies outlined by the European Commission.

      The carbon footprint must be declared, and eventually, batteries will have to meet maximum carbon footprint thresholds to be sold in the EU. This will drive a significant shift towards lower-carbon manufacturing processes and renewable energy use throughout the supply chain.

      ### Supply Chain Due Diligence

      The regulation requires economic operators to implement robust due diligence policies to identify and mitigate risks in their supply chains. This includes addressing issues such as child labor, forced labor, and severe environmental degradation.

      This is a major shift from voluntary guidelines to mandatory legal requirements. Companies must now proactively map their supply chains, assess risks, and take verifiable action to address them. This will require unprecedented transparency and cooperation from suppliers at every tier.

      ### Recycled Content Mandates

      To promote a circular economy, the regulation mandates minimum levels of recycled content for certain critical raw materials. By 2031, new batteries must contain 16% recycled cobalt, 85% recycled lead, 6% recycled lithium, and 6% recycled nickel. These targets increase significantly by 2036.

      These mandates will drive significant investment in battery recycling infrastructure and technologies. They also require robust systems to track and verify the origin and percentage of recycled materials in new batteries.

      ### Performance and Durability

      The regulation also sets strict requirements for the performance and durability of batteries. This ensures that batteries sold in the EU meet high standards for quality and longevity, reducing waste and protecting consumers.

      Manufacturers must declare the battery's expected lifetime, capacity, and other performance metrics. These metrics must be verified and accessible via the battery passport.

      ### The Role of Digital Technologies

      The implementation of the battery passport relies heavily on digital technologies. This includes robust data management systems, secure data exchange protocols, and potentially, blockchain or other distributed ledger technologies to ensure data immutability and traceability.

      Companies must invest in the IT infrastructure necessary to collect, manage, and share the required data securely and efficiently. This will require significant integration with existing enterprise resource planning (ERP) and supply chain management systems.

      ### Enforcement and Penalties

      Member states are responsible for enforcing the regulation and setting penalties for non-compliance. These penalties can be severe, including significant fines and the restriction or prohibition of the battery's sale in the EU market.

      The European Commission is also establishing a central electronic register to monitor compliance and ensure a coordinated approach to enforcement across the EU. This underscores the seriousness with which the EU is approaching this regulation.

      ### Navigating the Complexity

      The EU Battery Regulation is one of the most complex and far-reaching pieces of environmental legislation ever enacted. It requires a fundamental shift in how batteries are designed, manufactured, and managed.

      Companies must begin preparing now. This requires a comprehensive understanding of the requirements, a robust gap analysis, and the development of a strategic roadmap for compliance. The cost of non-compliance is simply too high.

      This page serves as your hub for understanding these requirements. We have broken them down into detailed, actionable guides. We rely exclusively on official EUR-Lex documentation, ensuring you have the most accurate and up-to-date information.

      ### Strategic Implications

      Beyond the immediate compliance requirements, the EU Battery Regulation carries profound strategic implications for the global battery industry. It sets a new global standard for sustainability, essentially forcing manufacturers worldwide to adapt if they wish to access the lucrative European market. This "Brussels effect" will likely accelerate the transition to more sustainable and transparent supply chains globally.

      Furthermore, the regulation will likely spur innovation in battery design and recycling technologies. As companies strive to meet the stringent recycled content mandates and carbon footprint thresholds, we can expect significant advancements in materials science, manufacturing processes, and closed-loop recycling systems. The battery passport itself will become a valuable source of data, enabling more efficient end-of-life management and potentially creating new business models based on the reuse and repurposing of batteries.
