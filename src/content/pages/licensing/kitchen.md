  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is a commissary kitchen letter of agreement?"
    text_content: |
      A commissary kitchen letter of agreement is a legally binding document signed by both the catering operator and the owner of a licensed commercial kitchen, proving to the health department that you have an approved facility for food preparation. It outlines the specific days, hours, and storage space allocated to the caterer, ensuring compliance with local food safety codes.

      Health departments will not issue a catering permit without this document. Home kitchens fall under cottage food laws, which explicitly ban the preparation and sale of potentially hazardous foods (like raw meat, dairy, or cooked grains) for off-site catering. The commissary letter proves you have access to commercial-grade refrigeration, three-compartment sinks, and proper grease disposal.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you evaluate a shared kitchen for catering?"
    text_content: |
      You evaluate a shared kitchen for catering by verifying its current health inspection score, checking equipment availability during your required hours, and confirming adequate dry, cold, and frozen storage space. You must also review the lease terms for hidden fees related to cleaning, waste disposal, or utility surcharges that can destroy your profit margins.

      A beautiful kitchen is useless if you cannot access the ovens during peak weekend prep times. Shared kitchens operate on tight schedules, and caterers need significant square footage for staging and packing out events. Ensure the facility has a designated area for loading and unloading catering vans without cross-contaminating prep areas.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What are the sanitation requirements for catering facilities?"
    text_content: |
      Sanitation requirements for catering facilities mandate a commercial dishwashing setup (either a high-temperature machine or a three-compartment sink), separate handwashing stations, mop sinks for wastewater disposal, and easily cleanable, non-absorbent surfaces. The facility must also maintain an active pest control program and pass regular, unannounced health department inspections.

      Because caterers transport food, the commissary must also support safe pack-out procedures. This includes designated clean zones for sanitizing transport equipment (like hot boxes and cambros) upon return from an event.

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Can a restaurant kitchen double as a catering commissary?"
    text_content: |
      A restaurant kitchen can double as a catering commissary only if the local health department reviews your specific prep volume and determines the facility has enough capacity (storage, sinks, and prep tables) to support both the restaurant menu and the catering operation simultaneously without risking cross-contamination.

      Many restaurants try to launch a catering wing without realizing their walk-in cooler cannot legally hold an extra two hundred plated meals. You will often need to submit a revised HACCP plan and floor plan to the health department for approval.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What equipment must a catering commissary provide?"
    text_content: |
      A catering commissary must provide a commercial dishwashing sink or high-temperature machine, dedicated handwashing sinks, a mop sink for wastewater disposal, and adequate commercial refrigeration that maintains temperatures below 41°F. It must also have sufficient dry storage space protected from pests.

      For caterers, the ability to store hot boxes, cambros, and chafing dishes is equally important. Ensure the commissary agreement explicitly outlines the square footage allotted for your equipment, as leaving items in shared aisles violates fire and health codes.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How often do health departments inspect commissary kitchens?"
    text_content: |
      Health departments inspect commissary kitchens routinely, typically one to four times per year depending on the local risk categorization and complaint history. During these inspections, the inspector will verify that all caterers operating out of the facility are legally permitted and adhering to strict time and temperature controls.

      If the commissary owner fails their inspection due to systemic issues like broken water heaters or pest infestations, all caterers operating under that roof may be temporarily shut down until the primary facility is remediated.
