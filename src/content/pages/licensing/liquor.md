  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What is an off-premise catering liquor license?"
    text_content: |
      An off-premise catering liquor license allows a business to legally serve alcohol at a location other than a licensed physical establishment. This type of permit is distinct from a standard tavern or restaurant liquor license because it covers temporary service at third-party venues or private property. Operating without it exposes the caterer to severe fines and criminal liability.

      Unlike permanent bars, caterers must adapt their alcohol service to different environments constantly. This includes securing the perimeter, checking IDs at varying points of entry, and adhering to the specific alcohol service cutoff times dictated by local ordinances for temporary events. Caterers often work under a primary master license but must apply for special event extensions for each gig.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How do you apply for a special event liquor permit?"
    text_content: |
      You apply for a special event liquor permit by submitting an application to your state or local liquor control board at least thirty to sixty days before the event date. The application usually requires proof of a primary off-premise catering license, the event floor plan, a security plan, and host permission. Some jurisdictions also demand written approval from the local police precinct.

      Timing is the most critical factor. State liquor boards (like the ABC or SLA) operate with strict deadlines, and late applications are rarely expedited. The process often involves a fee per event day and restrictions on the types of alcohol permitted (e.g., beer and wine only versus full liquor).

  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What are dram shop laws and host liability?"
    text_content: |
      Dram shop laws and host liability are legal doctrines that hold alcohol servers and businesses financially responsible if they serve an intoxicated person who subsequently causes injury or property damage. For caterers, this means you can be sued if a guest leaves an event and causes a drunk driving accident, making rigorous staff training and proper insurance mandatory.

      To mitigate this risk, caterers must carry specialized Liquor Liability Insurance. General liability policies typically exclude alcohol-related incidents. Staff must be trained in accredited programs like TIPS or ServSafe Alcohol to recognize signs of intoxication, cut off guests safely, and document incidents thoroughly to provide a legal defense.

      Return to our [Catering Licensing Requirements hub](/licensing/) for more on overall compliance. To understand the venue side of the equation, review our [Venue Agreements guide](/contracts/venues/).
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What kind of insurance is required for off-site liquor service?"
    text_content: |
      The required insurance for off-site liquor service is a Liquor Liability Policy, which specifically covers damages and legal fees if an intoxicated guest causes harm. General liability insurance almost universally excludes alcohol-related incidents, meaning a separate policy or rider is mandatory to protect your business assets.

      Without this coverage, a single lawsuit from a drunk driving incident involving a guest you served could bankrupt your catering company. The cost of this insurance varies based on your total annual alcohol sales and your claims history.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Can clients provide their own alcohol for catering?"
    text_content: |
      Clients can provide their own alcohol for catering in some jurisdictions, often referred to as a BYOB (Bring Your Own Beverage) event, but the caterer may still hold liability if they provide the bartenders serving it. If your staff is pouring, your liquor liability insurance and training requirements still apply.

      In many states, if the client buys the alcohol, it cannot be sold to guests (no cash bar). It must be an open bar at a private event. Always verify local regulations, as some health and liquor departments strictly forbid caterers from serving unverified alcohol.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Do you need a separate license for beer and wine?"
    text_content: |
      You do not always need a separate license for beer and wine, but many states offer a tiered permit system where a beer and wine catering permit is significantly cheaper and faster to obtain than a full liquor license. If your event volume primarily consists of corporate lunches or daytime weddings, opting for the lower tier can save thousands in annual fees.

      However, if a client requests a signature cocktail at an event where you only hold a beer and wine permit, you must legally refuse. Attempting to sneak hard liquor under a lower-tier permit will result in immediate revocation of your catering license.
