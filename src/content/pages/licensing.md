  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "What defines an off-premise catering license?"
    text_content: |
      An off-premise catering license enables a business to prepare food in an approved commercial facility and serve it at a secondary location. Operating legally requires more than a standard retail restaurant permit. You must satisfy local health department rules for mobile food transport, secure a commissary kitchen letter of agreement, and obtain temporary event permits for large-scale operations.

      Restaurant laws differ fundamentally from catering laws. While a restaurant is inspected at a fixed address, a caterer's operation is scrutinized from prep to transport to on-site service. Food must be kept out of the danger zone (40°F to 140°F) during transit, requiring commercial cambros and temperature logs.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "Do you need a commissary kitchen to cater?"
    text_content: |
      Yes, almost all health departments require a commissary kitchen for catering operations. A commissary is an approved, permitted commercial kitchen where you prepare food, store equipment, and dispose of wastewater. Home kitchens (cottage food laws) strictly prohibit catering high-risk foods like meat and dairy.

      To get licensed, you will need a signed commissary agreement detailing your scheduled hours and storage space.
  - _name: "TextBlock"
    heading: "How does an off-premise liquor license work?"
    text_content: |
      An off-premise liquor permit allows you to serve alcohol at a location other than your licensed physical establishment. This requires navigating host liability, dram shop laws, and often securing a one-day special event permit from the state liquor authority. Serving alcohol off-site demands rigorous staff training (like TIPS or ServSafe Alcohol) to prevent serving minors or visibly intoxicated guests.

      [Read more about liquor licensing](/licensing/liquor/)
