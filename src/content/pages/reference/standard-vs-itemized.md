  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: What is a Deduction?
    text_content: >-
      A tax deduction reduces your taxable income, which lowers the amount of tax you owe. The IRS gives you a choice: you can either take a flat-rate "Standard Deduction" or you can list out ("Itemize") your specific qualifying expenses. You are allowed to choose whichever method results in a lower tax bill.

  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: 2024 Standard Deduction Amounts
    text_content: >-
      For the 2024 tax year (filed in 2025), the IRS increased the standard deduction due to inflation:

      - **Single:** $14,600
      - **Married Filing Jointly:** $29,200
      - **Head of Household:** $21,900

      *Note: There is an additional standard deduction for taxpayers who are 65 and older or blind ($1,550 for married taxpayers; $1,950 for single/head of household).*

  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: When Should You Itemize?
    text_content: >-
      You should only itemize if your total deductible expenses are **greater** than your standard deduction. Since the standard deduction was nearly doubled by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017, nearly 90% of taxpayers now take the standard deduction.

      Common Itemized Deductions include:
      - State and local taxes (SALT) up to a $10,000 cap.
      - Mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of mortgage debt.
      - Charitable contributions.
      - Medical expenses that exceed 7.5% of your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI).

  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: Worked Example
    text_content: >-
      **Scenario:** John is single in 2024. His standard deduction is $14,600.

      His expenses for the year:
      - State income taxes paid: $4,000
      - Mortgage interest paid: $6,000
      - Charitable donations: $1,500
      - **Total Itemized Expenses: $11,500**

      **Result:** Because John's itemized expenses ($11,500) are less than his standard deduction ($14,600), he should take the standard deduction. Itemizing would actually cost him money.

  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: Self-Employed Exceptions
    text_content: >-
      If you are a freelancer or contractor with [1099 income](/reference/1099-misc-vs-nec), you **do not** need to itemize to write off your business expenses.

      Business expenses (like a home office, mileage, or software subscriptions) are deducted on Schedule C *before* your Adjusted Gross Income is calculated. You can deduct your business expenses on Schedule C AND still take the standard deduction on your 1040.

  - _name: TextBlock
    heading: FAQs
    text_content: >-
      **Q: Can I deduct my student loan interest if I take the standard deduction?**

      A: Yes. Student loan interest is considered an "above-the-line" deduction (an adjustment to income). You can deduct up to $2,500 (subject to income limits) even if you take the standard deduction.


      **Q: Do I need receipts if I take the standard deduction?**

      A: No. If you claim the standard deduction, you do not need to prove expenses to the IRS (unless you have business expenses on Schedule C or above-the-line deductions).
