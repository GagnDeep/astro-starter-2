When you choose to type your name instead of drawing it for an [electronic signature](/glossary#electronic-signature), the font you use sends a subtle message about your professionalism.

While the [ESIGN Act](/legality/us-esign-act) states that any mark or symbol indicating intent is legally binding, choosing a font that looks like a signature adds a layer of formality to the document.

In this guide, we'll break down the best fonts for typed signatures, how they affect legal validity, and common mistakes to avoid.

## Does the font affect legal validity?

The short answer is **no**.

Whether you type your name in Comic Sans or a beautiful cursive script, the legal validity of the signature under laws like the [US ESIGN Act](/legality/us-esign-act) and [EU eIDAS](/legality/eu-eidas) depends on two things:
1. **Intent to sign:** Did the signer deliberately type their name to agree to the contract?
2. **Audit Trail:** Can you prove the signer was the one who typed it? (This is why [usignpdf](/) attaches a cryptographic [Audit Trail](/security/audit-trails) to every document).

However, from a psychological and professional standpoint, the font matters immensely.

## Common Mistakes When Typing a Signature

1. **Using ALL CAPS:** A script font in all capital letters is entirely illegible. Always use Title Case.
2. **Using Non-Script Fonts:** Typing your name in Arial or Times New Roman doesn't feel like a signature. It just looks like part of the text body.
3. **Forgetting the Audit Trail:** As mentioned, the font doesn't make it legal. The metadata does. Never rely on just pasting a picture of a font into a Word document and calling it a signed contract.
