If you put a 1080p, 5GB `.mp4` file on your website, a user with a gigabit fiber connection will click play and have a great experience. A user on a 3G mobile connection on a train will click play, watch the loading spinner for two minutes, and then leave.

This is the fundamental problem of web video: network conditions are wildly unpredictable.

The solution, invented in the late 2000s and now powering Netflix, YouTube, and Twitch, is **Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR)**.

## The Core Concept

Instead of serving one massive file, ABR relies on two core principles:
1. **Multiple Renditions:** Encode the source video into several different quality levels (e.g., 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p).
2. **Segmentation:** Chop every single one of those renditions into tiny, bite-sized files (usually 4 to 6 seconds long).

When the player starts, it downloads a text file called a **Manifest**. The manifest lists all the available qualities and the URLs for every tiny 4-second chunk.

## How the Player Adapts

Let's walk through a real-world scenario of a user on a train.

1. **Initial Load:** The player downloads the manifest. It doesn't know the user's internet speed yet, so it plays it safe. It requests the first 4-second chunk of the lowest quality (360p).
2. **Speed Test:** The player measures exactly how long it took to download that first chunk. If the 4-second chunk downloaded in 0.5 seconds, the player knows the internet is fast.
3. **Scaling Up:** For the next chunk (seconds 4 through 8), the player looks at the manifest and requests the 1080p version.
4. **The Train Enters a Tunnel:** The user's cell signal drops drastically. The player attempts to download the next 1080p chunk, but it's taking too long. It is going to buffer.
5. **Scaling Down:** Before the buffer runs out, the player aborts the 1080p request and quickly asks for the 480p version of that exact same 4-second timestamp. The video quality drops slightly, but the playback never stutters.

## HLS vs DASH

There are two major protocols that govern how these manifests and chunks are formatted.

### 1. HLS (HTTP Live Streaming)
Developed by Apple in 2009 to allow video to play on the original iPhone without killing the network.
* **Manifest format:** `.m3u8`
* **Segment format:** Historically `.ts` (MPEG-TS), now heavily moving toward fragmented MP4 (`fMP4`).
* **Support:** Natively supported in Safari (macOS/iOS). Requires a JavaScript engine (like `hls.js`) to play in Chrome and Firefox.

### 2. MPEG-DASH
Developed as an open-source alternative to Apple's proprietary format.
* **Manifest format:** `.mpd` (XML based)
* **Segment format:** Fragmented MP4 (`fMP4`).
* **Support:** Relies entirely on Media Source Extensions (MSE) in JavaScript. Not supported natively in iOS Safari (which heavily restricts Apple devices from using it without complex workarounds).

Because of Apple's iron grip on the iOS ecosystem, **HLS has essentially won the streaming wars**. If you can only afford to package your video in one format, it must be HLS.

## The Engineering Cost of ABR

Implementing ABR is incredibly compute-intensive.

If you want to support a 5-rung ladder (1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p, 240p), your servers have to transcode the original video 5 separate times. Then, a packager must slice those 5 files into thousands of individual segments and generate the master manifest.

If you are a solo developer, building this pipeline using FFmpeg and AWS MediaConvert is a massive undertaking.
