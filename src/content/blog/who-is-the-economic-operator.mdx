# Who is the Economic Operator?

When reading the EU Battery Regulation (2023/1542), the phrase "economic operator" appears hundreds of times. Every major obligation—from calculating the carbon footprint to hosting the Digital Product Passport—falls squarely on the shoulders of the economic operator.

But in a complex, globalized supply chain involving cell manufacturers, pack assemblers, contract manufacturers, and vehicle OEMs, who exactly is the economic operator?

## The Legal Definition

The regulation defines several types of economic operators: the manufacturer, the authorized representative, the importer, and the distributor.

However, the critical legal threshold is **"placing on the market"** or **"putting into service."** The entity that first makes the battery available on the EU market is the one bearing the primary compliance burden.

### Scenario 1: The EU-Based OEM
If a German automaker buys cells from Asia, assembles them into a battery pack in Germany, and places that pack into a car to be sold in the EU, the **German automaker is the economic operator**. They are acting as the manufacturer of the final product and are responsible for generating the passport.

### Scenario 2: The Direct Importer
If a French distributor buys finished, boxed e-bike batteries from a manufacturer in China and imports them to sell to European repair shops, the **French distributor is the economic operator (as the Importer)**. Even though they didn't build it, they are placing it on the market. They must ensure the Chinese manufacturer provides all the necessary data to create the passport. If the Chinese manufacturer refuses, the French importer cannot legally sell the batteries.

### Scenario 3: The Contract Manufacturer
If you are a contract manufacturer in Eastern Europe building battery packs exactly to the blueprints of a UK brand, and the UK brand sells them under their own name, the **UK brand is the economic operator**. You are merely a supplier providing a service. However, the UK brand will absolutely require you to provide your primary manufacturing energy data to them.

## The Liability Trap

The most dangerous position to be in is the "unwitting importer." Many mid-sized European companies import light means of transport (like e-scooters) or industrial energy storage systems.

Under the new regulation, you cannot simply trust a CE mark sticker on the box. As the importer placing the product on the market, if market surveillance authorities scan the QR code and the required carbon footprint or due diligence data is missing, **you** face the fines and the product recalls, not the overseas factory.

## What to do next

1. **Review your supply chain:** Diagram exactly where title and liability transfer for the batteries you handle.
2. **Assign Responsibility:** If you determine you are the economic operator, you must immediately set up the IT infrastructure for the Battery Passport.
3. **If you are a supplier:** Even if you aren't the economic operator, your customers will demand this data. If you cannot provide a JRC-compliant carbon footprint, your European customers will drop you for a supplier who can.
