# Blockchain vs. Centralized Battery Passport: Which is better?

As the 2027 deadline for the Digital Battery Passport (DPP) approaches, economic operators face a critical architectural decision: Should the 90+ data attributes required by the EU Battery Regulation be stored in a centralized database or a decentralized blockchain ledger?

This post breaks down the technical tradeoffs, security implications, and compliance realities of both approaches.

## The Compliance Mandate

Before evaluating technology, we must understand the legal requirement. The EU Battery Regulation (2023/1542) mandates:
1. **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC):** Different stakeholders (public, recyclers, regulators) must see different data.
2. **Immutability:** Critical data (like carbon footprint and manufacturing origin) must be verifiable and tamper-evident.
3. **Interoperability:** The system cannot be a proprietary silo; it must use open standards (like JSON-LD) so third-party auditors and customs agents can access it seamlessly.

Crucially, the regulation **does not mandate blockchain**. It mandates a "decentralized data architecture" where the responsibility for data accuracy remains with the economic operator placing the battery on the market.

## Option 1: The Centralized Database Approach

In this model, a manufacturer uses a traditional relational (SQL) or document (NoSQL) database hosted in a cloud environment (AWS, Azure) to store all passport data. The QR code on the battery links to an API endpoint hosted by the manufacturer.

### The Pros
* **Speed and Cost:** Building a standard web API backed by PostgreSQL is significantly cheaper and faster than deploying a blockchain node network.
* **Familiarity:** Every IT department understands how to manage, backup, and scale a traditional database.
* **Access Control:** Implementing RBAC is straightforward using standard OAuth2/OIDC patterns.

### The Cons
* **Single Point of Failure:** If the manufacturer's server goes down, the passport is inaccessible.
* **Trust:** A centralized database requires auditors and consumers to inherently trust the manufacturer not to alter historical data (e.g., retroactively changing a carbon footprint score).
* **Supply Chain Integration:** Forcing Tier-2 and Tier-3 suppliers to log into the manufacturer's proprietary system creates immense friction.

## Option 2: The Blockchain (Decentralized Ledger) Approach

In this model, the data (or cryptographic hashes of the data) is written to a distributed ledger. Vendors like [Circulor](/vendors/circulor/) and Minespider champion this approach.

### The Pros
* **Immutability:** Once a supplier writes their Scope 3 emissions data to the ledger, it cannot be altered without detection. This provides mathematical proof to Notified Bodies during an audit.
* **Shared Truth:** A blockchain acts as a single source of truth across a fragmented supply chain. An automaker, a cell manufacturer, and a lithium miner can all write to the same ledger without needing to trust each other's private databases.
* **Data Ownership:** Through Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI), suppliers can maintain control over their proprietary data, granting access to the OEM only when necessary.

### The Cons
* **Complexity and Cost:** Deploying and maintaining enterprise blockchain networks (e.g., Hyperledger Fabric, Corda) is expensive and requires specialized engineering talent.
* **Privacy Concerns:** Public blockchains are entirely unsuitable for storing trade secrets. Even on private permissioned blockchains, careful architecture is required to ensure competitors cannot infer production volumes from transaction metadata.
* **The "Garbage In, Garbage Out" Problem:** A blockchain only proves that data hasn't changed since it was entered. It does not prove the data was accurate when it was entered. If a supplier lies about their energy mix on day one, the blockchain simply immortalizes that lie.

## The Emerging Consensus: The Hybrid Approach

Industry standards bodies, including the Battery Pass Consortium, are gravitating toward a hybrid architecture.

In this model:
1. **Heavy Data Storage:** The actual data payload (CAD files for dismantling, detailed chemistry breakdowns) remains in **centralized, off-chain databases** controlled by the respective suppliers or the economic operator.
2. **Trust and Verification:** A **blockchain or public ledger** is used solely to store decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and cryptographic hashes of the off-chain data.
3. **The Resolver:** The QR code on the battery points to a resolver service. The resolver checks the requester's credentials, fetches the data from the centralized database, and compares its hash against the blockchain to prove it hasn't been tampered with.

This provides the speed and privacy of centralized systems with the auditability of a blockchain.

## Worked Example: Updating State of Health (SoH)

Consider the dynamic requirement to update the battery's State of Health (SoH).
* **Centralized:** The vehicle's Battery Management System (BMS) pushes the SoH to the manufacturer's server every month. Simple, but vulnerable to tampering if the manufacturer wants to hide rapid degradation.
* **Blockchain:** Writing SoH updates to a blockchain every month for millions of vehicles would be prohibitively slow and expensive (transaction fees).
* **Hybrid:** The SoH is stored on the manufacturer's server. Once a year, an aggregated hash of the vehicle fleet's SoH data is anchored to a blockchain for regulatory auditing.
